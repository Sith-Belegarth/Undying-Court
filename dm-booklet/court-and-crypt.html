Part Three: The Court in Ruin (Dining Hall / Arena)

Other, smaller drains are impassable. The party may exit the sewers into the dining hall, where the next large drain is located, to explore the ruins of the court. There are hints throughout of the royals' perversions.

Quick version: Once the monarchs' great blood-sport arena, its stone benches now sag under decades of moss and silt. A long dining table sits set with corroded silverware, as if a feast were interrupted mid-course and never resumed.

Expanded, if you have the room for it: Once the monarchs' great blood-sport arena, its stone benches now sag under decades of moss and silt. The long dining table nearby is set with corroded silverware, as if a feast were interrupted mid-course and never resumed. Rusted manacles hang from the arena's support columns.

DM note: the mosaics clue from the Antechamber pays off directly here. This is what they depicted.

Suggested Content

  • A Barbarian's hook payoff: a single surviving ancestral trophy rack, empty but for one weapon. The ancestral blade the Fighter's warlord wants, or a hint toward it.
  • One easy roleplay/investigation beat: a preserved menu or ledger (Investigation DC 12) naming the "guests" who never left, seeding NPC names.
Moderate

Undead Arena Champions. The arena's dead answer the call to fight once more. Statues and shackled remains alike wrench free of moss and rust, taking up whatever's left of their old weapons (x2 champions). Fittingly for the room, they don't rise silently — the crowd's roar seems to echo with them.

Statline: reskin the two remaining Broken Guardians from the Antechamber (see Part One — Animated Armor stat block, AC 18, HP 33 (6d8+6), Multiattack: two Slam attacks, 1d6+2 each) as arena champions rather than temple statues. Same stats, new flavor.

This is the party's second taste of the animated guardians — a full pair now, after the single warning shot in the Antechamber. Drawn by noise from the sewers onward.

Scaling Note

Two champions swing from Moderate (party of 3, level 3) down to Easy (party of 6, level 5).

The Inner Courtyard (Optional, off the Dining Hall)

A genuine branch, not a detour that gates progress — skip this room entirely if there's no Ranger or Druid in the party. A door off the Dining Hall, half-swallowed by ivy, leads out to what was once a formal courtyard. It didn't stay formal.

Quick version: Open sky, for the first time since the party went underground. A small wood has reclaimed the courtyard — real trees, real undergrowth, pushing up through cracked flagstones. It doesn't belong here, and it doesn't feel like it wants to.

Expanded, if you have the room for it: The corridor opens onto open sky — the first the party has seen since the antechamber. What was once a formal courtyard has become a small, wild glade: real trees, thick undergrowth, moss climbing walls that were clearly built to keep it out. Birdsong, faint and strange, drifts from somewhere in the canopy. The air smells of green growing things instead of rot, which after everything else in this dungeon feels almost more unsettling than reassuring.

What's here. At the glade's center stands a crimson stag — a genuine fae creature, its coat the color of old blood, antlers branching like cracked lightning. A manacle of cold iron rings its neck, chained to a stake driven into the earth. The stag doesn't struggle. It watches the party arrive with an intelligence that makes clear this is not an animal.

Ranger Hook

This is the crimson stag the Ranger's hook describes. Legend undersold it — this isn't a monster to hunt, it's a captive to free or leave chained. Let the Ranger's player recognize what they're looking at (no roll needed if the hook is theirs); anyone can identify it as fae in nature with a DC 13 Nature or Arcana check.

Freeing the Stag

The manacle is cold iron — a material that suppresses fae magic on contact, which is exactly why it's holding a creature this powerful. Removing it is simple in principle (it isn't locked, just heavy) and takes an action; no check required. The moment it comes off, the stag doesn't run. It changes.

The crimson coat brightens, the cracked-lightning antlers steady, and for a moment the stag isn't an animal at all — it's a door. A figure steps through where the stag stood, tall and unhurried, dressed like the forest itself decided to attend court. Oberon inclines his head to the party, unbothered, as if he'd been expecting them.

Oberon's Offer. He is courteous, faintly amused, and entirely uninterested in violence — he came for his stag, not a fight, and he'll say as much if asked. He offers a boon in thanks, tailored to whoever's actually in the room:

  • If a Warlock is present (particularly one bound through the Raven Queen, like Malrik Dhoran), Oberon offers to cleanse the corruption from their patron's connection and transfer their pact to him instead — Hexblade to Archfey. He's honest about the terms: the cleansing takes time. He can begin the working now, but it completes in a year and a day. The Warlock can accept the offer today and carry the promise forward, or decline outright. There's no take-back once accepted, and no rush to decide — Oberon is patient in the way only an immortal can afford to be.
  • If a Druid is present, Oberon already knows what's wrong with this place. He offers the means to cleanse the Ritual Hall's altar — not just permission, but the actual method: a working, or a small vial of something silver and cold, whichever you'd rather hand over. This is the concrete "how" behind the Druid's altar-cleansing hook (see the Ritual Hall, below); without this, the Druid can still attempt the DC 15 check unassisted, but Oberon's gift removes the roll entirely and makes the healing certain rather than merely possible.
  • If neither is present, Oberon still offers something — a minor boon to the party as a whole (a Potion of Superior Healing, a single-use ward against the Court's undead, or a favor redeemable later in a longer campaign). He doesn't leave empty-handed generosity on the table; it's a matter of pride.
Design Note

Play Oberon as genuinely unbothered by the Court, the dungeon, or anything the party has done so far — he's older than all of it and treats the whole affair as faintly beneath him. That tone is the point: after three parts of rot, sconces, and undead, a fae lord who's mildly delighted to have his stag back is a real tonal breath, the same way the Sunken Hearth was.

If the Party Attacks

Oberon does not fight. He was never going to. If the party attacks the stag, attacks him, or tries to force the manacle off in a way that reads as hostile rather than freeing, he simply leaves — stepping back through the same door he arrived by, the crimson stag at his side, gone before anyone can land a blow. He takes no offense he'll act on personally; he just declines to stay somewhere he's not welcome.

He doesn't leave the party nothing, though. Oberon travels with an attendant — a fey creature bound to his court and, unlike him, entirely willing to fight. He leaves her behind on his way out, a parting consequence rather than an accident: she steps from among the trees where the stag stood, and she has no interest in letting the party leave quietly.

The Urns

Set apart from the rest, on the trophy rack itself, two sealed urns flank the empty space where the ancestral blade should be — older, plainer, and unmistakably out of place among the Court's gilt and glasswork. Anyone who's already been through the Sunken Hearth will recognize the shape immediately (no check needed); anyone who hasn't can identify them with a DC 13 Arcana or Religion check, the same skills used on the Antechamber's mosaics — grave urns, carried up from somewhere below and never returned.

Serai Hook

If Serai Thalos is in the party, let her be the one to place it — this isn't an abstract desecration to her.

Opening either urn (no check) reveals ash, disturbed and picked through rather than left whole — someone came looking for something specific.

  • A DC 10 Investigation search of each urn turns up 350 sp and 40 gp respectively, coin and small effects left behind by whoever did the searching, not the original grave goods.
  • A DC 15 Arcana check identifies why: both urns are missing exactly the components a necromancer would want from cremated remains — bone dust, ash for certain conjuration rituals. Nothing here was taken for its monetary value. It was taken to be used.
Design Note

This gives you a clean, ugly answer if anyone asks why urns from a sealed satyr crypt are sitting in a human dining hall: Malqor found the Sunken Hearth first, and it didn't stay sealed for him. Consider letting this be the moment a Warlock or Cleric with an altar hook, or Serai with her Bond, first suspects Malqor specifically rather than "the Court" abstractly — it's a good seed for the crypt confrontation ahead.

The Treasury (Side Room, off the Dining Hall)

Place it as a locked or hidden side door off the Dining Hall or off the corridor leading to the Ritual Hall, whichever suits your table's pacing.

Design Note

Three separate hooks — Rogue, Wizard, and Sorcerer — all point at a treasury or vault. This is that room; it fits the Rogue's "paid for vault access" hook directly. It doesn't need to be large — a single vault chamber the party can choose to detour into is enough to pay off all three hooks without padding the dungeon.

Design Note

This room is the direct payoff for the Foreshadowing callout in Part One: the sconce crystals and the sword crystals are confirmed the same substance, and this is where that substance (and the knowledge to work it) actually turns up. If you'd rather not add a room, redirect all three hooks to the crypt loot instead (see Treasure below, Part Four) — just be explicit with the players that the crystals they've been tracking are down there, so the hook still resolves.

Design Note: Path of Darkness Made Flesh

If the party's Monk already practices the Way of the Long Death (as Shaen Orvo's pregen does), a night spent meditating on the scroll — a full short or long rest, uninterrupted — deepens her understanding of her own tradition. Award a small, table-appropriate bonus: a flat XP award as if for a minor milestone, an extra 1st-level Ki-adjacent feature for the session, or (if your table runs milestone leveling) treat it as confirmation she's ready for her next level, fictionally justified rather than earned through combat.

If the party's Monk practices a different tradition, meditating on the scroll overnight (same conditions: a full short or long rest, uninterrupted) grants them the Way of the Long Death subclass, replacing or supplementing their existing Monastic Tradition at your table's discretion. If there's no Monk in the party, the scroll is still a real find — a named, infamous work within a specific monastic school, worth something to the right buyer or library, and a solid roleplay hook (Shaen would want to know it exists, whether or not she's the one who finds it).

DM Only — Do Not Read Aloud

This is an old scroll, and the Way of the Long Death was forbidden for a reason the text itself doesn't advertise. Its author didn't master death through meditation and philosophy alone — they captured living creatures and subjected them to elaborate, fatal experiments, recording and studying the precise moment of death in exacting detail. The tradition's mastery over death was built on a body count.

None of this needs to come up mechanically. It's context for you, not a plot hook you're obligated to spring — but it's worth having in your back pocket if a player asks what the tradition's history actually looks like, or if you want the gift to cost the Monk something narratively (a nightmare, an NPC's suspicion, a moment where their own path echoes the scroll's author a little too closely).

Part Four: Battle in the Crypt

This level contains the ruins of the throne room. It overlooks the dining hall and leads to a ritual hall where waking the preserved king and queen is depicted, and a crypt lair where the lich king and vampire queen await the rising of the next age of darkness.

The Ritual Hall

Blood-crusted grooves score the black granite altar at the room's center — an altar to Orcus, Prince of Undeath, and the source of the Court's corruption. If the party carries any hook that involves the altar (Cleric, Paladin, Warlock), this is where it pays off. The altar offers exactly two live interactions: feed it, or cleanse it. A party that does neither can simply walk past — nothing forces engagement.

DM Only — Table Safety Check-In

Feeding the altar is the module's heaviest optional content: a real offer to permanently transform a PC into a vampire or lich, and a possible scenario where party members are pressured or compelled to attack one another. None of it triggers without a player choosing to bleed on the altar. Before you run this scene, it's worth a quick out-of-character check with your table (or at least the player whose character is closest to the Cleric/Paladin/Warlock hook) that permanent character transformation and PvP-adjacent stakes are something they want. If not, the party can still interact with the altar meaningfully by cleansing it instead — see below — or simply leave it alone.

Feeding the Altar

The altar answers blood, not intent. A single drop is enough to trigger it — there's no verbal negotiation, no "are you sure" prompt. Offering blood is the agreement, and the character doing it should understand that going in, even if their player doesn't fully grasp what they're agreeing to yet. Make sure the read-aloud makes this unambiguous before anyone rolls or narrates cutting themselves.

The grooves in the altar aren't decoration. They're old blood, and they're waiting for more. Whatever this is meant to do, it wants something living to start.

The moment blood touches stone, the ceiling above the altar opens — not physically, but like a curtain drawn back. A portal, or perhaps only a very old mirror, shows a realm of bone and rot far away. Something vast looks back through it. It does not need to come closer to be terrifying.

Orcus's Offer

Orcus speaks through the portal — he never physically enters the room; he doesn't need to. He is amused, patient, and utterly certain of the outcome. He offers the bleeding character a place among his favored: remade as a vampire or a lich, a small portion of his own dominion over death made theirs to keep. In exchange, he asks only that they carry his name into the world and spread death in it.

This is not a trick dressed as a gift — it's a genuine offer, played straight. If the character accepts, the transformation is real, permanent, and mechanically significant. See Accepting the Pact, below.

Accepting the Pact

If a player wants this for their character, treat it as a real alternate character path, not a punishment:

  • Vampire. Reskin the transformation using the SRD Vampire Spawn as a mechanical floor — the character keeps their class and levels, but gains the Spawn's Regeneration, a bite attack that deals necrotic damage and grapples, and the standard vampiric weaknesses (sunlight, running water, no reflection). Work with the player on how much of this replaces their existing kit versus layers on top; this is a session zero-style conversation, not something to resolve in the middle of combat.
  • Lich. Reskin using the SRD Mage the same way Malqor Veyth is built (see The Crypt, below) — archmage energy in an undead husk, not the full CR 21 Lich block. The character keeps their class; they gain a phylactery (a real, findable object now, and a real vulnerability later), resistance to necrotic damage, and immunity to being frightened.
  • Either way, this is a campaign-level commitment, not a one-shot flourish. If you're running this as a standalone session, talk to the player about what happens after the table stands up — whether this becomes session one of something longer, or whether you handle the transformation narratively and move on.

If the Party Splits

This is the scenario the table-safety check-in above exists for. If one or more characters accept Orcus's offer and others in the party refuse or object, Orcus is direct about what happens next: his new servant proves their devotion by dealing with the others. He orders them killed on the altar — sacrificed the same way they were, willing or not.

  • If the newly-turned character (with or without help) succeeds in killing a party member on the altar, that character doesn't simply die — they rise again as a mindless zombie, bound to the altar's will rather than Orcus directly. This is a real, permanent loss of that PC as written; talk to that player about a replacement character or an NPC ally filling the gap.
  • If the rest of the party wins the fight instead — killing or subduing the newly-turned character before Orcus's order is carried out — the pact is broken, but the altar remains active and furious. Cleanse it quickly (see below) or Orcus will retaliate: raising the recently dead nearby, or sending a wave of undead after the party in a future session as an escalating grudge.

You do not need to force this confrontation into the same combat round as the offer. It's fine — often better — to let the tension sit for a scene or two before it turns violent, especially if the party is still arguing about what just happened.

Cleansing the Altar

The alternative to feeding it is shutting it off for good. Cleansing doesn't just tidy up the corruption — done right, it consecrates the altar and locks Orcus out of it entirely, permanently closing the portal/mirror above it. This is the Druid's hook payoff, and it's also open to a Cleric or Paladin who'd rather consecrate the altar than feed it.

Druid / Cleric / Paladin Hook

A 10-minute ritual, no roll needed if a Cleric or Paladin performs it (a Consecrate-style rite is exactly their wheelhouse), or DC 15 Religion or Nature for a Druid working alone. If the party received Oberon's gift in the Inner Courtyard, this requires no roll at all — his gift is specifically built to make this certain. The immediate effect is quiet: the blood-crusted grooves lighten, the air in the room eases, and the portal above the altar seals shut, this time for good.

The real payoff is downstream and slow, particularly for the Druid — the corruption spreading into the natural world (their hook) begins to heal once its source is cleansed rather than fed. Tell that player plainly: this is the moment their hook resolves, even though the change won't be visible until later, outside the dungeon.

Beyond the altar itself, the room hasn't been picked clean: warped shelves line one wall, a scorched flask sits where it was left, and a lantern long since out of oil still hangs from a bracket. Worth a look before the party moves on.

The Study (Secret Room off the Ritual Hall)

Finding it. A section of the Ritual Hall's wall, behind the altar, doesn't match the rest of the stonework. Subtly newer, subtly less worn. A DC 15 Investigation check (actively searching) or DC 17 Perception (passive, if you describe the wall as "off" and let a sharp-eyed player bite) reveals a seam. The mechanism is a twist on one of the altar's carved grooves, not a lever. A DC 12 Investigation or Arcana check to work out how it opens once found, or it can simply be forced (DC 18 Strength, loudly).

Quick version: A narrow door, cut flush into the stone, swings inward on a hinge that hasn't rusted like everything else here. Beyond it: a small room, dry and undisturbed — no moss, no rot.

Expanded, if you have the room for it: A narrow door, cut flush into the stone, swings inward on a hinge that hasn't rusted the way everything else here has. Beyond it: a small room, dry and undisturbed. No moss. No rot. Whatever kept the rest of the Court decaying for centuries never got in here.

What this room is. Malqor's private study, sealed off from his own household before the binding. This is a place even Seraphyne's court didn't have access to. It's the one room in the dungeon frozen rather than ruined: the dust is even, the air is still, nothing has rotted.

Design Note

This should feel wrong in a way the party can sense even before they know why. This is a good spot to let Perception/Investigation-minded players notice the absence of decay before you explain it, rather than telling them outright.

Contents. A writing desk, a single shelf of texts, and a locked strongbox. The spellbook and the crystal-enchantment notes are the point of this room. Finding the desk means finding them, no roll required. Everything else on the desk is worth a proper search.

The Crypt: Boss Encounter

Deadly

By the numbers, Malqor + Seraphyne together are Deadly for a level 3 party at any size 3–6. That's intentional for a boss fight, but it's also why the two-phase structure and lair action below matter — give the party the Phase 1 warning window rather than opening with both bosses at full strength.

Scaling Note

"Deadly at every size" hides a big real difference — the same two bosses are far closer to a TPK against 3 level-3 characters than against 6 level-5 characters, even though both register as Deadly on paper. Use this table to right-size the fight to your actual table before running it.

PartySuggested Adjustment
3 players, level 3This is the module's hardest matchup. Extend Phase 1 (bound at the altar) to 3 rounds instead of 2, and consider letting the party arrive with a short rest already banked if they cleared the Study or Treasury without a fight first.
4–5 players, level 3Run as written.
6 players, level 3Run as written, or add one legendary-action-style bonus action to Malqor (an extra Ray of Sickness at disadvantage) to keep pace with the extra actions arrayed against him.
Any size, level 4Add Malqor's 3rd-level slot spells (he's already a 9th-level caster on the Mage block — use them) and consider a lair action every round instead of only past round 3.
Any size, level 5The fight is Hard, not Deadly, at this tier — add a third combatant (reuse a Broken Guardian/Champion stat block as a bound guard who frees itself in Phase 2) if you want the boss room to still feel climactic.

Lich King Malqor Veyth (King-Consort of Ossyrra) and Vampire Queen Seraphyne Thalussyrr (Queen of Ossyrra) wait at the center of the crypt, entombed but not destroyed.

  • Malqor Veyth was once a titled member of Ossyrian elite, an ambitious necromancer, and one of the few among the living upper class who supported Queen Seraphyne's continued rulership after her undeath.
  • Seraphyne Thalussyrr survived a vampire attack on her family's castle; forever changed, her undeath left her the only survivor. She was determined to retain her crown when many sought to invalidate her claim because of her undeath.

A note on stat blocks: don't scale down the actual Lich (CR 21) or Vampire (CR 13) for this fight. Even "weakened," those blocks assume abilities and complexity built for a much higher-level fight, and trimming legendary actions on the fly mid-session is a recipe for a rough table. Instead, both bosses below are reskins of lower-CR blocks that already match the behavior the script calls for — no improvised nerfing required.

Malqor Veyth — "Lich King"

Archmage energy trapped in an undead husk. Use the SRD Mage stat block as-is, reflavored: swap damage types to necrotic and reskin the spell list (chill touch instead of fire bolt, etc.). This reads as lich-adjacent for the table without importing lich-specific mechanics like Turn Resistance, Rejuvenation, or legendary actions.

CR6
AC12 (15 w/ mage armor)
HP40 (9d8)
Speed30 ft.
Attack+5 to hit (spell)
Save DC13

Reflavored Spells. Chill Touch (as fire bolt, necrotic, prevents healing on hit), Ray of Enfeeblement or Blight (as cone spells), Vampiric Touch or a necrotic-flavored fireball-equivalent for the big AoE. Keep the Mage's spell slots/cantrips as printed — only reskin damage type and descriptions.

Role in the fight: ranged control and area denial. Stays at range, uses the room's sconces as free actions (see below), retreats behind Seraphyne if approached in melee.

Seraphyne Thalussyrr — "Vampire Queen"

Use the SRD Vampire Spawn stat block as-is — no reflavoring needed. It already grapples and drains, which is exactly the melee behavior the script calls for.

CR5
AC15
HP82 (11d8+33)
Speed30 ft., climb 30 ft.
Attack+5 to hit (claws/bite)
Damage1d8+3 slashing / 1d6+3 + 2d6 necrotic (bite, grapple only)

Regeneration. Regains 10 HP at the start of its turn if it has at least 1 HP and isn't in sunlight or running water. Role in the fight: closes into melee immediately, grapples and drains the party's weakest-looking member, exactly as scripted.

Running the fight: This is your boss room, so treat it as a two-phase encounter rather than a straight stand-and-swing.

Phase 1: Awakening. The party enters mid-ritual. Malqor and Seraphyne (stat blocks above) begin bound to the altar and can't move for the first 2 rounds. Use this window for a warning volley (necrotic bolts, spectral hands) rather than full attacks.

Phase 2: Free. Once unbound, both act at full strength. Malqor leans on ranged necrotic spells and commands the room's crystal-powered sconces as free actions (reuse the sconce stat block). Seraphyne closes into melee and tries to grapple/drain the party's weakest-looking member.

Lair action (round-end, no roll needed): the crypt's ceiling groans. Trigger one Falling Rubble hazard automatically each round the fight continues past round 3, escalating the pressure to end things.

Treasure (The Crypt)

The Dais — The Ossyrran Blade. At the crypt's center, where Malqor and Seraphyne were bound, a longsword hilt rests in a shallow groove cut for it. A single black crystal is socketed into the pommel. This is the Ossyrran Blade — no check required, they've earned it reaching this room. Print the Ossyrran Blade handout and hand it to the Fighter's player.

The Ossyrran Blade

A longsword hilt, rare, requires attunement (see below). While grasping the hilt, you can use a bonus action to cause a blade of solidified energy to spring into existence, or make the blade disappear. While the blade exists, this magic longsword has the finesse property. If you are proficient with shortswords or longswords, you are proficient with this weapon. You gain a +2 bonus to attack and damage rolls made with this weapon.

The hilt itself is inert. A plain, unremarkable grip that could pass for mundane. Its power comes entirely from a single crystal socketed into the pommel, and attunement is to the crystal, not the hilt. A creature attuned to a crystal can freely move it between any compatible hilt; a creature holding the hilt without being attuned to its crystal gains none of the weapon's benefits, though the blade will still spring into existence for them (dim, guttering, deals no damage). Enough to identify what they're holding without letting an unattuned wielder use it.

Swapping crystals. Removing a crystal from a hilt takes an action and doesn't damage it. Attuning to a different crystal follows the normal attunement rules. The creature must end a short or long rest focused on the new crystal, and can only be attuned to one Ossyrran crystal at a time.

The Chest — The Treasury. Bound in blackened iron, sealed beside the dais as a monarch's grave goods would be. Inside, resting in fitted velvet slots clearly built to hold more than what remains: five uncut crystals (white, red, green, purple, and cyan) alongside 300 gp and 75 pp in mixed coin and loose gems (roughly 75 gp).

Quick Escape. From the crypt, the party may find a secret, hidden exit that shortcuts them to the outside. Useful if you want a "grab the win and run" ending rather than a full clear.

Design Note

Don't let the module force a clean kill. If Malqor or Seraphyne is reduced to 0 HP but the party doesn't destroy the phylactery / the coffin isn't staked-and-burned, either monarch can return later as a recurring antagonist. Perfect if you want this one-shot to become session one of something longer.
